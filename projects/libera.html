Transparency of confiscated mafia assets

A data visualisation project with Libera, the Italian association against organised crime, as part of LUISS’s GAP2 activities. Libera provided the data; we cleaned it and built a dashboard to explore it.

Context

Many assets confiscated from organised crime in Italy are transferred to municipalities, which are required to publish a list of those assets and how they are being reused. Libera’s RimanDATI monitoring checks whether municipalities actually do this, and how well.

Data

  • RimanDATI, from Libera’s national report on the transparency of confiscated assets: one row per municipality, with the number of assets transferred, whether a list is published, its date and format, and eleven yes/no checks on its contents (cadastral data, address, size, intended use, whether reuse is specified, the manager, the concession act and its duration). After matching municipalities to provinces and coordinates we had 969 municipalities and 14,332 transferred assets.
  • Confiscated companies: the ANBSC and Unioncamere open-data releases, merged into one table of 2,950 companies with legal form, sector (ATECO code), location and status.
  • Region and province boundaries from openpolis’ GeoJSON, used for every map.

What the data shows

602of 969 municipalities had not published the list
219published as searchable PDF, the most common format
38%of confiscated companies still active
503companies in construction, the largest sector

Of the municipalities that did publish, many used formats that are hard to reuse: 44 image-only PDFs and 34 images, against 22 spreadsheets and 4 CSV files. Among the companies, Sicily (946), Campania (508), Calabria (377) and Lazio (373) account for most of the total; after construction the most common sectors are bars (135) and real-estate trading (133).

The dashboard

Four pages in Streamlit:

  • Maps: transferred assets by province and region, classed with Jenks natural breaks so a few very large values don’t flatten the rest of the scale; and a selector across the transparency checks showing how each region contributes to the national total.
  • Publications over time: an animated cumulative count of published lists and a breakdown by file format.
  • Trends: for any transparency check, how the counts of “yes” and “no” evolved month by month, plus a bar-chart race of publications by region.
  • Confiscated companies: top sectors, active against ceased companies by sector, distribution by region and status, and maps of the predominant sector in each region and province.

A design question we had to answer

Raw percentages mislead when regions differ wildly in size: a region with three municipalities that all comply would top a ranking ahead of one with two hundred. The dashboard explains this on the page and shows each region’s contribution to the national count. In hindsight I would show both the share and the within-region rate side by side, since they answer different questions: where most compliance happens, and where it is most likely.
